## 0. Introduction

> Part 10 of the series. The day I finished build 10, the visitor entry, which is part of build stage 6 (connecting to the outside). The server-side preparation that went in earlier the same day (the platform server account, admission policies, private network access rules) did not get a post of its own; it is one line in [Woowangthon 0](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-00-overview/#7-where-we-are-now). The previous post is [part 9](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-09-student-space/).

The school PCs sit behind the school [firewall](glossary:firewall). I needed a way for visitors to reach `https://demo.stairnoworks.com` without asking the school to open a port.

In restaurant terms, the school PCs are the inside of a kitchen that outside guests cannot open the door to. So the kitchen first lays a private passage out to a front desk outside, and guests take that passage from the front desk to the table a hall staff member points to. With a single staff member every guest is cut off whenever that person takes a break, so there are two. The front desk is [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare), the private passage is a [Cloudflare Tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel), the hall staff is [Traefik](glossary:traefik), and the table is the student app's [Kubernetes Service](glossary:kubernetes-service).

I opened `https://demo.stairnoworks.com` without opening a single inbound port to the school. The leader dials out to [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) first, and visitor requests ride that [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) to [Traefik](glossary:traefik) on the [workers](glossary:worker-node). Then I turned off a [worker](glossary:worker-node) and cut the leader's internet. I expected about half of the requests to fail when the [worker](glossary:worker-node) went down; for 80 seconds all of them did. And a [self review](glossary:code-review) caught a wrong claim the agent had written, plus a test namespace name that could collide with a student's. Stage 6 still has the platform server joining the private network and the deploy executor on the leader, and stage 7 is monitoring.

## 1. The way in for visitors

The school PCs sit behind the school [firewall](glossary:firewall). Nobody outside can open a connection to them first, and we decided not to ask the school to open a port. So, as planned in the [overall structure](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-00-overview/#5-overall-structure), the school PCs open the connections outward.

| Leg                                                                     | What happens                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Visitor → [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare)                             | [HTTPS](glossary:https). [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) issues the certificate                                                          |
| [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) → leader                              | Requests come in over the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) the leader's [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) opened earlier (port 7844) |
| Leader → [Traefik](glossary:traefik) on [workers](glossary:worker-node) | Plain [HTTP](glossary:http). [Traefik](glossary:traefik) takes outside requests and hands them to student apps by address (the hostname)   |
| [Traefik](glossary:traefik) → student app                               | The [Service](glossary:kubernetes-service) in the student space `proj-<slug>`                                                              |

The domain is `stairnoworks.com`, which I already own. Its DNS was already on [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare), so there was nothing to buy or move. One [wildcard record](glossary:wildcard-dns-record), `*.stairnoworks.com`, points at the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel). Student addresses are one level down, like `<slug>.stairnoworks.com`, so [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare)'s free certificate covers them with [HTTPS](glossary:https). A new student address adds one [Ingress](glossary:ingress) for [Traefik](glossary:traefik) to read; DNS, the certificate and the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) stay as they are.

I weighed other ways in. Making an [AWS](glossary:aws) server the public entrance and forwarding to the [workers](glossary:worker-node) over the private network sends every visitor through [AWS](glossary:aws), which is slower, and if that server goes down the student services go with it. Path-based addresses like `stairnoworks.com/<name>` break student apps that assume they live at the root path. The decision is recorded as [ADR](glossary:adr)-013 in the team repo.

## 2. One Traefik on each worker

[k3s](glossary:k3s) installs [Traefik](glossary:traefik) by default. When I checked, there was only one, on [worker](glossary:worker-node) 2. If [worker](glossary:worker-node) 2 went down, every student address would go down even with the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) up.

The [Traefik](glossary:traefik) that [k3s](glossary:k3s) installs can be overridden value by value with a HelmChartConfig. Two replicas, student [workers](glossary:worker-node) only, never two on the same node ([pod anti-affinity](glossary:pod-anti-affinity)). It is the same shape as [CoreDNS on both workers](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-06-k3s-install/#5-coredns-on-both-workers).

I applied it and waited, and the rollout never finished. The chart's default strategy starts a new [Pod](glossary:pod) first ([maxSurge](glossary:rolling-update) 1) and removes the old one afterwards ([maxUnavailable](glossary:rolling-update) 0). But there are two [workers](glossary:worker-node) and each already has a [Traefik](glossary:traefik). The [pod anti-affinity](glossary:pod-anti-affinity) rule leaves the new [Pod](glossary:pod) nowhere to go. It would wait forever, until a third node showed up.

So I flipped the order: take one down first, then start the new one in its place ([maxSurge](glossary:rolling-update) 0, [maxUnavailable](glossary:rolling-update) 1). During a rollout, a single [Traefik](glossary:traefik) carries the load.

![Traefik Pod list. Before: one on worker 2. After: one each on worker 1 and worker 2. The last line shows maxSurge 0, maxUnavailable 1](/static/images/woowangthon-10-public-entry/traefik.svg)

_Before and after. The numbers in the wait loop are replicas / ready / updated_

## 3. The tunnel hands over plain HTTP

Between the visitor and [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) it is [HTTPS](glossary:https), but when the leader's [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) hands a request to [Traefik](glossary:traefik) it is plain [HTTP](glossary:http). Encryption ends at [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare). Left alone, a student app believes it was called over http. An app that forces [https](glossary:https) then sends a visitor who already came in over [https](glossary:https) to [https](glossary:https) again, and the visitor ends up in a [redirect loop](glossary:redirect-loop).

[cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) passes the visitor's original details along in headers such as `X-Forwarded-Proto: https`. The catch is that [Traefik](glossary:traefik) doesn't trust these headers from just anyone, since anyone can make them up. So I made [Traefik](glossary:traefik) trust them **only from the leader's [Pod](glossary:pod) range (`10.42.0.0/24`)**, which is where [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) lives.

I checked with the test app (podinfo). podinfo has a `/headers` endpoint that echoes back the request headers it received.

![demo.stairnoworks.com from the desktop. https 200, an unknown name 404, http also 200. Headers the app received: X-Forwarded-Proto https, X-Forwarded-Port 443. The certificate is the *.stairnoworks.com wildcard, issued by Let's Encrypt](/static/images/woowangthon-10-public-entry/demo.svg)

_The visitor address (203.0.113.10) is a masked value_

The app receives `X-Forwarded-Proto: https`. Unknown names get [Traefik](glossary:traefik)'s 404. `http://` also returns 200 because [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare)'s "Always Use [HTTPS](glossary:https)" setting is still off. It is a single domain setting, to be turned on separately.

## 4. Creating the tunnel without showing its secret

I created a locally managed [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel). Its routing rules (everything under `*.stairnoworks.com` goes to [Traefik](glossary:traefik), the rest gets 404) live in a config file in the repo, not in the [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) dashboard. Changes leave a PR behind.

Creating a [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) needs a [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) login, and the login is approved in a browser. The browser is on my desktop at home, not on the leader. So I created the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) on the desktop. The domain is mine, so I approved the login myself.

```text
$ cloudflared tunnel create wanggane
Created tunnel wanggane with id <tunnel id>
$ cloudflared tunnel route dns wanggane '*.stairnoworks.com'
INF Added CNAME *.stairnoworks.com which will route to this tunnel tunnelID=<tunnel id>
```

Creating the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) leaves a credentials file. It is the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel)'s only secret. To move it to the leader, I didn't copy it as a file; I piped it over [SSH](glossary:ssh) straight into a [Kubernetes Secret](glossary:kubernetes-secret).

```bash
ssh k3s-leader 'sudo k3s kubectl -n cloudflared create secret generic tunnel-credentials \
  --from-file=credentials.json=/dev/stdin' < ~/.cloudflared/<tunnel id>.json
```

I compared only the size on both sides (175 bytes), then deleted the credentials file and the login certificate (`cert.pem`) on the desktop. The contents never appeared on screen. Nothing is left in the repo or on the desktop.

## 5. UDP 7844, which nobody had checked

[cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) connects to [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) on port 7844. It uses both [TCP](glossary:tcp) and [UDP](glossary:udp), and if [UDP](glossary:udp) works it connects with [QUIC](glossary:quic). [In part 2 we planned to check in stage 3 whether 7844 got through the school network](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-02-network-design/#5-putting-a-status-on-every-line), and [part 5 only checked TCP](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-05-node-prep/#1-the-last-site-checks). Nobody had measured [UDP](glossary:udp) since. If it were blocked, [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) would fall back to [TCP](glossary:tcp) ([HTTP/2](glossary:http2)), so it wasn't a big deal, but I wanted to know which one we would get.

```text
INF Initial protocol quic
INF Registered tunnel connection connIndex=0 ... location=icn07 protocol=quic
INF Registered tunnel connection connIndex=1 ... location=icn06 protocol=quic
INF Registered tunnel connection connIndex=2 ... location=icn06 protocol=quic
INF Registered tunnel connection connIndex=3 ... location=icn07 protocol=quic
INF |  UDP Connectivity  region1.v2.argotunnel.com  PASS    QUIC connection successful    |
INF |  TCP Connectivity  region1.v2.argotunnel.com  PASS    HTTP/2 connection successful  |
INF |  SUMMARY: Environment is healthy. cloudflared will use 'quic' as primary protocol.  |
```

It just worked. On the first run, four [QUIC](glossary:quic) connections registered in Seoul (icn06, icn07). The one thing in this stage that ended without a fight.

## 6. Turning off a worker: all, not half

Time to break things. On [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1, where the test app was running, I stopped [k3s](glossary:k3s) and every container (`k3s-killall.sh`, with a restart scheduled 240 seconds later). From the desktop, I called the demo address once a second.

The plan expected this: during the [50 seconds it takes for a worker to be marked NotReady](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-06-k3s-install/#notready-takes-50-seconds-not-40), requests are split between the two Traefiks, so the half that lands on the dead [worker](glossary:worker-node)'s [Traefik](glossary:traefik) fails. That is not what happened.

![Worker-off test. No answer (000) from 0 to 50 seconds, 503 from 54 to 78 seconds, 200 from 80 seconds. On the leader, worker 1 turns Unknown at 49 seconds and a new Pod appears on worker 2 at 78 seconds. cloudflared times out on a cluster DNS lookup](/static/images/woowangthon-10-public-entry/worker-off.svg)

_Desktop probes and leader-side state, with 0 seconds at the moment [k3s](glossary:k3s) was stopped_

**From 0 to 50 seconds, everything failed.** For two reasons.

- There was only one test app, and it was on the dead [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1. Even the [Traefik](glossary:traefik) on the healthy [worker](glossary:worker-node) 2 sent requests to the app on [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1. Two Traefiks don't help when there is one app.
- [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) finds [Traefik](glossary:traefik) by name (`traefik.kube-system.svc.cluster.local`). That lookup goes to [cluster DNS](glossary:coredns), and [CoreDNS](glossary:coredns) also runs one per [worker](glossary:worker-node), so half the lookups went to the [CoreDNS](glossary:coredns) on the dead [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1. Those timed out.

A dead node's addresses stay in the lists until it is marked [NotReady](glossary:node). For those 50 seconds, requests kept going to the dead side, for [Traefik](glossary:traefik) and [CoreDNS](glossary:coredns) alike.

**From 50 to 78 seconds, [503](glossary:http-status-code).** At 49 seconds [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1 turned [NotReady](glossary:node) (Unknown, to be exact) and its addresses were removed. The app's address list was now empty, so [Traefik](glossary:traefik) answered [503](glossary:http-status-code), "nowhere to send this". When I first wrote this up I labeled this stretch [502](glossary:http-status-code); going back to the raw log, it was [503](glossary:http-status-code). I corrected it.

**From 80 seconds, 200.** [Kubernetes](glossary:kubernetes) waited 30 seconds after [NotReady](glossary:node) and started a new app on [worker](glossary:worker-node) 2 at 78 seconds. The gap visitors saw was about 80 seconds: 50 for [NotReady](glossary:node) plus 30 of waiting before giving up on the node.

One more thing. The replacement [Traefik](glossary:traefik) and [CoreDNS](glossary:coredns) stayed Pending until [worker](glossary:worker-node) 1 came back. With the [pod anti-affinity](glossary:pod-anti-affinity) rule, [worker](glossary:worker-node) 2 had no room left. Until then, one of each carried the load. Worker 1 was Ready again 34 seconds after [k3s](glossary:k3s) started back up.

If the app had been on the surviving [worker](glossary:worker-node), only about half would have failed, as planned. I didn't measure that. Student apps run one per project, so when a [worker](glossary:worker-node) really goes down, the students on it lose about 80 seconds outright, as here.

## 7. Cutting the leader's internet: 502, then 530

Next, the leader's internet. I can't pull the wall LAN cable from home, so I ran a script that removes the leader's default route for 120 seconds and puts it back. The nodes still talk to each other; only the outside is cut. In case the script failed to restore the route, I also scheduled a reboot 7 minutes out. Remote work starts with [the undo in place before it breaks](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-05-node-prep/#3-put-the-undo-in-place-before-it-breaks).

![Internet-cut test. Route removed at 6 seconds, restored at 126. The leader-side connection count metric drops from 4 to 1 and never reaches 0. On the desktop: 502, then 530 from 58 seconds, 200 at 137 seconds](/static/images/woowangthon-10-public-entry/internet-cut.svg)

_cf is [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared)'s connection count metric, ts is the [Tailscale](glossary:tailscale) state, route is whether the default route exists_

For roughly the first 50 seconds visitors got [502](glossary:http-status-code): [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare) still believed the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) connections were alive and sent requests that got no answer. After that, [530](glossary:http-status-code), [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare)'s response once it decides the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) is offline. When the route came back, [Tailscale](glossary:tailscale) and [SSH](glossary:ssh) returned in 3 seconds, the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) re-registered in 9, and the demo was 200 after 11. No one had to touch anything.

Two things caught my eye in the logs.

First, [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) had been restarted 58 seconds into the cut. The [liveness probe](glossary:liveness-probe) saw no connection and restarted the container. With a 120-second cut that did no harm. But with a cut of several minutes the restarts repeat, and the [kubelet](glossary:kubelet) lengthens the wait before the next restart every time (up to 5 minutes). When the internet comes back, [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) is still sitting in that wait and reconnects that much later. [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) reconnects on its own, so I removed the [liveness probe](glossary:liveness-probe) and kept only the [readiness probe](glossary:readiness-probe) (`/ready`).

Second, [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared)'s connection count metric (`cloudflared_tunnel_ha_connections`) read 1 for the whole cut. It never dropped to 0. For the next stage (monitoring) I had planned a "[tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) down" alert on this metric hitting 0, which would have stayed silent through a cut like this one. I changed the design doc to alert on `/ready` or on [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare)'s side instead.

Cuts longer than a few minutes, and pulling a real cable, are still unmeasured. That waits for a day in the club room. Moving the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) to both [workers](glossary:worker-node), so the visitor path stays open when the leader is down, is deferred. Right now, if the leader goes down, new deploys and visitors are cut together.

## 8. Two things the self review caught

I opened the PR ([woowangthon/WOOWANGTHON_TEAM_18#91](https://github.com/woowangthon/WOOWANGTHON_TEAM_18/pull/91)), which is waiting for a teammate's approval, and ran a [self review](glossary:code-review) (the agent's [code review](glossary:code-review)). It found seven things, and I fixed all of them. Two are worth writing down. Both came from the agent I built this stage with.

### The leader's firewall doesn't stop cloudflared

The design doc said that "[cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared)'s outbound traffic is narrowed by [the leader's 7844 firewall rule](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-05-node-prep/#outbound)". That was wrong. That rule only applies to programs running directly on the leader. [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) is a [Pod](glossary:pod). [Pod](glossary:pod) traffic leaves by a different path (FORWARD), where a rule allows the whole [Pod](glossary:pod) range. [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) could go anywhere.

This is [exactly what the design doc review in part 5 had already caught](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-05-node-prep/#7-what-the-design-doc-review-caught). We wrote down back then that "once the [tunnel](glossary:cloudflare-tunnel) program runs as a [Pod](glossary:pod), it is wide open regardless of this rule", and on the day it actually became a [Pod](glossary:pod), the same mistake came back in.

[cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) is the only process that takes requests from the internet, and it runs on the leader. If it were compromised, it would be in reach of the [k3s](glossary:k3s) API, the kubelets and the image cache. So the [cloudflared](glossary:cloudflared) namespace got its own outbound rule ([NetworkPolicy](glossary:networkpolicy)): [cluster DNS](glossary:coredns), the [Traefik](glossary:traefik) [Pods](glossary:pod), and ports 7844 and 443 on the internet minus private ranges, nothing else. I probed it from a test [Pod](glossary:pod).

```text
traefik svc :80 (demo host) -> exit=0 200
k3s API 10.43.0.1:443 -> exit=7 000
leader API :6443 -> exit=7 000
image cache :5000 -> exit=7 000
kubelet w1 :10250 -> exit=7 000
vllm svc -> exit=7 000
internet 443 -> exit=0 200
internet 80 -> exit=7 000
```

Only the allowed targets are reached; everything else is refused right away (exit code 7).

### The test namespace `proj-check`

[The test script from part 9](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-09-student-space/#5-how-do-you-know-its-blocked) creates a second student space called `proj-check` and deletes it at the end. While setting the student address rules this time, I noticed that `check` is a perfectly valid student name. If a student created a project called `check`, it would collide with the test script's space, and the end of a test run could delete that student's space.

I renamed the test space to `proj--check`. A student name can't start with a hyphen, so no input can ever produce this name. That is more reliable than adding `check` to a reserved list. Someone can forget to update a list; there is nothing to forget about a name the rules can't produce.

## 9. Server addresses on the same domain

The first plan only put student addresses on this domain. The platform's web and server addresses were tied to open question 5 in the system definition (how far to connect the domain, and who pays for buying one), so the agent left them as "for the team to decide" and kept them out of this stage.

I looked at that question again. The domain is mine, its DNS is already on [Cloudflare](glossary:cloudflare), and there is nothing to buy. The cost question had already been answered. Putting every student address on this domain while leaving the platform's web and server on long [AWS](glossary:aws)-issued addresses made no sense. I decided on the spot: the web at `app.stairnoworks.com`, the server at `api.stairnoworks.com`. Open question 5 is closed.

A record for a specific name takes precedence over the [wildcard](glossary:wildcard-dns-record), so on the day each deploy target ([CloudFront](glossary:cloudfront), [EC2](glossary:ec2)) exists, one record gets added. Until then the [wildcard](glossary:wildcard-dns-record) catches them and [Traefik](glossary:traefik) answers 404. Students must not use `app` or `api` as project names, so platform, mail and test names went into a reserved list. The server checks it when it saves a project.

It is the same shape as [the question of who sets the caps in part 9](/en/blog/hackathon/woowangthon/woowangthon-09-student-space/#2-who-sets-the-caps). The agent didn't cross a line marked "for the team to decide". Being careful is right. But you can't tell from that line alone whose decision it really is, or whether it has already been answered. The person who owns the domain and knows the team's situation can. This time, I was the one who asked.

## 10. Reflection

Don't stop at a line marked "for the team to decide"; go on to ask whose call it is.
