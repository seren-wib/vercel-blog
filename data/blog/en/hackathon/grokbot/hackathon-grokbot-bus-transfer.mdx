## 0. Introduction

> This is the story of the bus transfer bot I built in one day at Grok Bot Seoul Build Day on November 1, 2026. The code was written by "Jubi", a [Grok Bot](glossary:grok-bot) [agent](glossary:ai-agent). This post is about my side of the work: **what I told it to build and why**, and **how I made the results trustworthy**. I made the entry repo `bus-transfer-bot` private after the event, because it holds code that calls the internal [API](glossary:api) behind the Naver Map website, not an official [API](glossary:api) published by Naver.

A map app picks exactly one transfer point, and people who know their buses redo that math in their heads. I wanted to hand that mental math to a bot.

In restaurant terms, a head chef splits the orders and hands them to line cooks, each cook plates only their own share, and the head chef collects the plates and serves the guest. I wrote the recipes and the order-slip format and tasted what came out, and Jubi did the cooking. The head chef is the [orchestrator](glossary:orchestrator-worker-pattern), the cooks are the workers (search bots), the slips and plates are the files in the bot [contract](glossary:interface), written as [JSON](glossary:json), and the recipes are the [requirements specification](glossary:requirements-specification).

What I did: wrote the [requirements specification](glossary:requirements-specification) and the [contract](glossary:interface), changed the rules after the rehearsal, verified the results against Naver, Kakao and Google maps, and gave the three-minute pitch.

## 2. The problem

If you ride buses a lot, you know this moment. The map app says "get off here and transfer", you get off, and the next bus is 20 minutes away. The app picks exactly one transfer point and shows it even when the real wait there is long. So people who know their buses redo the math in their heads. The line I put on slide 2 of the deck is exactly that math (translated):

> That stop gets that bus at that time… and I'll get there around then, so oh, this one's faster?

The goal was to hand this mental math to a bot. I wrote the problem at the top of the [requirements](glossary:requirements-specification) document like this:

- The app recommends **only one** transfer point, and shows it as-is even when the real wait there is 20 minutes.
- Hubs such as transfer centers collapse several platforms into one stop.
- To find a faster transfer, a person has to search again from every stop and open real-time arrivals separately.

So the solution is one line too: **treat every stop the first bus passes as a transfer candidate, recompute each with real-time arrivals, and pick the one that gets you there first.**

## 4. The first design: an orchestrator, search bots, and a file contract

One [orchestrator](glossary:orchestrator-worker-pattern) splits the job into task files and hands them out, and each worker writes a result file for its own share only. The [orchestrator](glossary:orchestrator-worker-pattern) collects the result files and makes the final call.

![Orchestrator and worker structure. Input (GPS, destination) → orchestrator (R1 baseline route, R2 every stop of the first bus) → task files → search workers W1–W8 (R3 platforms within 500 m, R4 platform to B, R5 real-time arrival) → result files → orchestrator (R6 total time, R7 pick the minimum, never slower than the map apps)](/static/images/hackathon-grokbot-bus-transfer/pipeline.svg)

The task files are `tasks/bot{n}.json` and the result files are `results/{si}.json`. The structure is [orchestrator–worker](glossary:orchestrator-worker-pattern), the simplest form of a [multi-agent](/en/blog/cs-fundamentals/ai/ai-07-01-ai-agent/#5-single-agent-and-multi-agent) system.

What I decided in the first version, and why:

- **Bots talk only through files.** Only the [orchestrator](glossary:orchestrator-worker-pattern) writes `tasks/bot{n}.json`, and each search bot writes `results/{si}.json` **for its own stop only**. When several bots work in one repo at once, the first thing to break is overwriting the same file. Pin each file to a single writer and the conflict can't happen by construction.
- **A failure kills only one candidate (N3).** If one search fails, only that stop's result drops out and the overall calculation still runs. In a one-day event an external map service can slow down or block you at any moment, so I chose to keep partial results.
- **A search cap (N2) and a time cap (N4).** At first I capped it at 8 intermediate stops, 4 hub platforms, and 20 searches in total. If one run takes more than 5 minutes, the picture of "get a route from one sentence" falls apart. The plan was to measure one search in the rehearsal and reset the numbers.
- **No penalty for transfers (R6).** People do dislike transfers, but the question this bot answers is "when do I arrive". One criterion is what makes the result comparable to the map apps.
- **The demo is pre-run results plus screenshots; a live run is the backup (N5).** In a three-minute pitch, one stalled live search ends it.
- **The rehearsal checklist comes first.** Does the intermediate stop list show on screen, do real-time arrivals appear on the route screen, are hub platforms shown separately, how many seconds does one search take. Run the bots without knowing these and the whole design stands on assumptions.

## 5. Rules changed after the rehearsal

A rehearsal is the step where assumptions are checked before the main work. The 11:20 rehearsal (Sadang Station → Gwanghwamun) broke several of them, and I rewrote the [requirements](glossary:requirements-specification) at 13:30. The "change history" table at the end of the document is the record. Here are the changes with the reasons.

| Item                | Before                               | After                                                                                                          | Why                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Map app             | Pick one, drive its UI               | Naver Map web internal [JSON](glossary:json) [API](glossary:api) (unofficial); Kakao and Google for comparison | Kakao's route [API](glossary:api) was blocked by an anti-automation token. On Naver, one [API](glossary:api) call took 0.6–0.8 s versus 1–1.3 s on screen |
| Transfer candidates | Platforms inside a hub only          | Every platform within a 500 m walk                                                                             | Hub platforms were split by stop number (18+ at Sadang Station). We'd miss the case where a faster bus comes from the platform across the street          |
| Walking time        | Fixed 1 min inside a hub             | Computed from meters ÷ 83.3 m/min (5 km/h)                                                                     | At 500 m a fixed 1 min is wrong. Computing from meters instead of the app's minutes keeps all apps on the same basis                                      |
| Wait                | Real-time arrival of line Y          | Based on **when the user reaches that platform**                                                               | The bus arriving right now leaves before the user gets to that stop                                                                                       |
| Safety margin       | Under review                         | None, always the fastest                                                                                       | The question was fixed as "arrive the earliest"                                                                                                           |
| Comparison basis    | The app's top result only            | Best of Kakao, Naver, Google; **if slower, return the app's route**                                            | A bot that gives a slower answer than the app has no reason to exist. When it loses, it hands over the app's route (R7)                                   |
| Running lines       | None                                 | Lines not running now are removed from results entirely                                                        | The raw result of a Sunday 13:35 search included "새벽A741 (autonomous) (not on Sat/Sun)" departing at 05:09 the next day                                 |
| Bot roles           | Three search bots, same instructions | One Naver worker ([concurrent](glossary:concurrency) queries) + Kakao browser + Google script                  | With the [API](glossary:api), one query took under a second, so running queries [concurrently](glossary:concurrency) in one process beat adding more bots |
| Search cap (N2)     | 8 stops, 4 platforms, 20 searches    | The whole line (e.g. 15 stops), up to 5 platforms per stop                                                     | Same reason. As long as it finishes inside the 5-minute cap, looking at more is better                                                                    |
| Input               | Address of A                         | Current GPS, departure now                                                                                     | It has to end with one sentence: "I want to go to Gwanghwamun"                                                                                            |

The running-line filter took the most work. It removes lines whose names carry "not running", "weekdays only", "dawn" or a late-night N, lines whose service calendar doesn't include today, lines outside first-to-last bus, lines with neither real-time data nor headway info, and routes that board more than 120 minutes later. When a line had no real-time data, the wait was estimated as half of its headway. I pinned this rule down with `tests/test_filters.py`, a [test](glossary:unit-test) that checks 새벽A741 is dropped on a Sunday.

### Split off what code can do

At first the bot explored by opening the map apps in a browser itself. That took far too long. So I had the repeated parts (route search, real-time arrival lookup, stop and line lookup) split off into scripts, and used the bot only to call the scripts and interpret the results. Requirement N1 is that directive. Naver is fixed to `naver.py`, which calls the [JSON](glossary:json) [API](glossary:api) through the request context of a [Playwright](glossary:playwright) [headless browser](glossary:headless-browser), and never grabs and drives a desktop browser. Because this [JSON](glossary:json) [API](glossary:api) is not an official one, we called it only as much as the demo needed during the one-day hackathon.

The problem was getting the scripts into the bot. [Grok Bot](glossary:grok-bot) doesn't let you put script files into it directly. So I bundled the scripts (data collection and parser files) into a zip, uploaded it to Google Drive, and wrote in the bot's instructions to fetch and read that file. The bot did not run the files as-is. It read them and then wrote its code again the way they showed. In the end the zip worked not as executables but as **instructions written in code**. Showing the bot working code was a more precise instruction than describing at length how to call the Naver [API](glossary:api). The catch is that [Grok Bot](glossary:grok-bot) needs its Google Drive integration turned on.

## 6. Verification: at first, we lost

The first integrated run at 13:36 (GPS near Whimoon High School → Gwanghwamun, bus 740 as the first bus, 15 stops) came out slower than the map apps. It's recorded as-is in the repo's `docs/progress.md`.

- Our best: get off at Gangnam Station Exit 9 → walk 413 m → 741, **74.5 min**
- Naver bus-only 69–74 min, Kakao bus-only 68 min (suspicious: a late-night N bus in the daytime) and the 401 direct at 83 min, Google 401 direct about 53 min

This is where R7 did its job. When our candidate is slower than the apps' best, the bot recommends the app's route as-is. So the bot never confidently handed out a wrong answer. There's a note written alongside, too. Each map app puts the same 401 direct at anywhere from 53 to 83 minutes. Whether and when they include the real-time wait differs per app. So I added a rule: **record the query time with every comparison**.

### The numbers after that

The numbers below come from runs Jubi did in the afternoon and went onto slides 5–7 of the deck. **They are not in the repo; they are the bot's own measurements, sourced from Jubi's chat log.** I did not re-measure them, and Jubi once flipped its own [test](glossary:unit-test) verdict from FAIL to pass. So I only claim "this is what the bot measured".

| Measurement (2026-11-01, Sunday, bot's own)                 | Value                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GPS near Whimoon High School → Gwanghwamun, 14:15, bus only | Our bot 65 min · Naver 74 min · Kakao 84 min                                              |
| That route                                                  | Board 350 at Samseong Station Exit 3 → get off at Seocho Youth Library → walk 399 m → 470 |
| 10 random pairs in Seoul, queried 14:28–14:32               | Faster than Naver bus-only in 8/10 pairs, also 8/10 against Kakao's displayed time        |
| Time saved over 10 pairs (final recommendation)             | Mean 7.4 min, median 8.7 min                                                              |
| The 2 slower pairs (P07, P09)                               | Naver's route recommended as-is, per R7                                                   |
| 8 workers in parallel, 40 stops                             | 9.7 s until all finished (7.1–9.7 s per worker)                                           |

I put the limits of the comparison on the deck as well. Kakao shows only a duration with no departure time, so we used the time shown in the app, and since it leaves out the wait it was used for comparison only. Google Maps runs on timetables rather than real time, so it was left out of the 10-pair comparison. The three numbers are therefore not on exactly the same basis.

## 7. The pitch

The deck started from a draft Jubi made; I finished it by adding the Naver Map screenshots and quote on slides 2 and 3, the "where we got stuck" and "next steps" text, and the demo links. The pitch was 3 min 5 s, cut into 15 seconds per slide and 60 seconds of demo. The demo video is on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjsTcFMaYkE), from the single sentence "I want to go to Gwanghwamun" to the recommended route.

The deck lists four next steps: extend to two transfers, add the subway, factor in crowding to pick a comfortable ride, and learn each user's walking speed from GPS to fit the bot to them.

## 8. What I'd do differently

- **Keep measurements in the repo.** The strongest numbers in the deck (8/10, mean 7.4 min) are not in the repo. If they live only in a chat log, even I can't check them again. The [contract](glossary:interface) should have said from the start: [commit](glossary:commit) the run scripts and raw results to `results/`.
- **Fix the comparison basis first.** Each app handles waiting differently, so the basis of "N minutes faster" wobbled. Pin down the query time and whether the wait is included before measuring, and the numbers hold up.
- **Check first what the bot can take in.** I found out late that scripts can't be put in directly and bolted on the Drive workaround. It worked out because the code doubled as instructions, but what form of input the bot accepts is something to check on day one of the design.

## 9. Reflection

Had we moved to scripts the moment the rehearsal exposed the [API](glossary:api), there would have been more time in the afternoon for verification.
